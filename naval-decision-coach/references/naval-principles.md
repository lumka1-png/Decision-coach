# Naval's Decision Principles

Read this for every decision. These are the rules that sit on top of the four models and act as tie-breakers and sanity checks. Quotes marked as quotes are Naval's well-known phrasing; everything else is a paraphrase. Do not invent new quotes and attribute them to Naval.

## The foundation: clear thinking before analysis

> "Almost all biases are time-saving heuristics. For important decisions, discard memory and identity, and focus on the problem."

- **Discard memory**: past experience is useful, but on an important decision it can make you assume the future will copy the past. Sunk costs (time, money, emotion already spent) do not count toward the future.
- **Discard identity**: once you identify strongly with a group, a role, or a label, you stop thinking for yourself on topics that touch it.
- **Focus on the problem**: what is actually true now, and what actually matters?

## The three core decision rules

### 1. "If you can't decide, the answer is no."

When facing a big decision and you cannot choose, default to no. Modern life offers too many options; only say yes to things that clearly excite you. A hesitant yes usually turns into a commitment you resent.

**When this rule is right**: adding new commitments (a job offer, a partnership, a purchase, a move, a project, a relationship escalation) where the person feels lukewarm.

**When this rule can be wrong** (always check):
- When "no" means staying in something that is clearly harmful or declining (an abusive situation, a dying role, a bad habit). Here, "no" is not the safe default; it is a choice to keep a known bad outcome.
- When the hesitation comes from fear of the unfamiliar rather than real doubt about the option. Ask: "If you knew it would not fail, would you be excited?" If yes, the doubt is fear, not a lack of desire.
- When the window is closing and the option is cheap to reverse. Reversible decisions deserve a lower bar.
- When the person is chronically indecisive and uses "no" to avoid all growth. Then the rule becomes a shield for conservatism.

### 2. Run uphill.

When choosing between two paths that are roughly 50/50, take the one that is more painful in the short term. Short-term pain usually leads to long-term gain; the easy path is often the wrong one. (Naval often cites Jerzy Gregorek's line: "Easy choices, hard life. Hard choices, easy life.")

Check: is the harder path hard because it builds something (skill, ownership, honesty, health), or just hard? Running uphill means choosing productive difficulty, not suffering for its own sake.

### 3. Shed your identity.

Beliefs taken in packages are suspect. When you identify as part of a party, tribe, profession, or "type of person," you stop seeing reality clearly. Ask: "What would I choose if I had no label to protect?"

## Supporting principles

- **Play long-term games with long-term people.** All the returns in life, in wealth, relationships, and knowledge, come from compound interest. Choose options and partners you would want to still be with in twenty years.
- **Pick partners with high intelligence, high energy, and above all, high integrity.** Without integrity, the first two work against you.
- **Wisdom is knowing the long-term consequences of your actions.** Always ask what each option looks like in five or ten years, not just next month.
- **Own, don't rent.** "You're not going to get rich renting out your time." Prefer options that build equity, specific knowledge, and a reputation you own, with accountability for the results.
- **Specific knowledge.** Prefer paths that build knowledge that feels like play to the person but looks like work to others. It is hard to compete with someone who is being authentically themselves.
- **Never risk ruin.** No upside justifies a meaningful chance of permanent loss (health, freedom, reputation, all your capital).
- **Impatience with actions, patience with results.** Decide and act quickly on what is in your control; be patient with how long the results take.
- **Desire is a contract you make with yourself to be unhappy until you get what you want.** Check whether the person truly wants the goal, or whether they have picked up someone else's desire. Choose fewer desires and pursue them fully.
- **Peace of mind is a valid criterion.** Naval values a calm mind. An option that pays more but costs constant anxiety or obligation has a real cost; count it.
- **Inspiration is perishable.** If the person is genuinely excited about something reversible and low-risk, acting soon is often better than analyzing forever.

## Naval's recommended lenses

Naval suggests studying microeconomics, game theory, psychology, persuasion, ethics, mathematics, and computers. Each gives a new lens, and the person with many models sees patterns others miss. When you face a decision, cycle through the models: Does compounding apply? Is this a principal-agent problem? What would inversion reveal? Is this a repeated game?

## Quick tie-breaker checklist

Use when the analysis is close:

1. Is there a clear, excited yes? If not, lean no (then check the exceptions above).
2. If truly 50/50, which option is harder now but better later? Lean toward it.
3. Which option keeps the person in long-term games with people of integrity?
4. Does either option carry a real risk of ruin? Rule it out or add protection.
5. Which option builds something the person owns?
6. Which option would they choose with no identity or history to protect?
7. Which option gives more long-term peace of mind?
