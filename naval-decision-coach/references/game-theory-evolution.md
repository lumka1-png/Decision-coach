# Evolution and Game Theory

**The question it answers**: How should I act when the outcome depends on other people, or on a future I cannot predict?

**One-line summary**: Cooperation wins in repeated games. Variation and selection beat prediction.

Both models describe systems where your result depends on other players and on repetition. Game theory asks what to do when others are choosing at the same time as you. Evolution asks what survives when nobody can predict the future.

**Naval's rule, "play long-term games with long-term people," summarizes both models in one sentence.**

## Game theory basics (explain simply if needed)

- **Players**: the people or organizations involved.
- **Strategies**: the possible actions each player can take.
- **Payoffs**: what each player gets, depending on everyone's choices.
- **Nash equilibrium**: a situation where no player can do better by changing only their own strategy.

### The prisoner's dilemma

Two people each choose to cooperate or defect. Payoffs (you / them):

| | They cooperate | They defect |
|---|---|---|
| **You cooperate** | +3 / +3 (both build) | 0 / +5 (you are exploited) |
| **You defect** | +5 / 0 (you win, once) | +1 / +1 (both lose) |

- **One-shot game**: defecting pays. You take the +5 and never see them again. This is the logic of the tourist-trap restaurant.
- **Repeated game**: cooperating pays. +3 per round forever beats +5 once followed by +1 every round after. Reputation becomes an asset.

**Most people do not choose the wrong move. They misjudge which game they are playing.** Almost every relationship worth having (work, family, friends, clients, suppliers) is a repeated game. Most bad behavior comes from treating a repeated game like a single round.

### Tit for Tat (the winning strategy)

In 1980 Robert Axelrod ran a tournament of strategies for the repeated prisoner's dilemma. The winner, from Anatol Rapoport, was the simplest entry:

1. Start by cooperating.
2. After that, do whatever the other player did last round.

It won again in a second tournament, even after everyone knew it had won the first. Its four traits:
- **Nice**: never defects first.
- **Provocable**: responds to exploitation.
- **Forgiving**: returns to cooperation as soon as the other side does.
- **Clear**: predictable, so others can cooperate with it.

Lesson: cooperation can emerge from self-interest without altruism or a central authority. Caveat: Tit for Tat can get stuck in cycles of retaliation after an accidental defection, so forgive quickly and communicate.

## Evolution basics

Evolution does not predict. It runs a loop:

1. **Variation**: generate many cheap, small, different attempts. Quantity of attempts, not quality of the plan.
2. **Selection**: apply one measurable criterion, decided in advance. Reality decides, not the most senior or loudest person.
3. **Retention**: keep what survived, write down why, and make it the starting point for the next round.

Repeat the loop faster and more cheaply than others. **The cost of one experiment sets how many experiments you can run, which sets how fast you learn.**

- In a business: ten small pilots, not one large program.
- In a career: many small bets on skills and side projects.
- In a product: ship, measure, kill, repeat.
- In personal life: try a city for a month before moving; take a short course before a degree; freelance before quitting.

## Real examples to draw on

- **TSMC's promise not to compete with its customers**: turned a suspicious one-shot transaction into a long repeated game, so customers could share sensitive designs safely. The pledge is the business model.
- **Costco's markup cap and high wages**: gives up money this round to keep customers and staff in the game for many more rounds. Result: low turnover and very loyal members.
- **Jim Sinegal (Costco founder)**: refused to raise margins when analysts demanded it, because it would cost more later than it earned now.
- **Naval**: choose counterparties who will still be there in twenty years. Then cooperation becomes the obvious move. Selection of partners matters as much as strategy.

Cooperation in these cases is not just a moral position. It is the higher-paying strategy once the game repeats. And the winning strategies are simple and transparent; complexity mostly helps you defect, which is the losing move.

## Where it shows up

- **Negotiation**: a salary, supplier, or landlord negotiation is often a repeated game disguised as a single one. Winning every point today raises the price of every future round. Ask how many more times you will sit at this table before you decide how hard to push.
- **Workplace politics**: hoarding information or credit is a one-shot move inside a repeated system.
- **Pricing and competition**: a price war is the "both defect" square where everyone gets +1. Differentiation is how you leave it.
- **Family and friendships**: almost always repeated games with very long horizons.
- **Uncertain choices** (new career, new business, new city): nobody can predict the outcome, so run small experiments instead of making one big forecast.

## How to apply it to a decision

1. **Identify the game before choosing a move.** How many more times will they deal with this person or organization? More than a few means it is a repeated game, however the moment feels.
2. **Write down the other side's real payoff.** Not their stated position. What gets them paid, promoted, or protected? People act on payoffs, not arguments.
3. **Open with cooperation, visibly.** Make good faith easy to see; a cooperative move nobody notices earns nothing.
4. **Respond once, then forgive at once.** Tolerating defection teaches the other side it is free. Respond proportionally, once, then restart cooperation.
5. **Be predictable on purpose.** Clear, simple rules make cooperation possible.
6. **Where you cannot predict, experiment.** Many small trials, one success measure agreed in advance, drop the losers without ego, keep and document the survivors.

**Choose your games as carefully as your moves.** Prefer repeated games with long-term people, and decline anonymous one-shot games where possible.

## Diagnostic questions to ask the person

- "How many more times will you deal with this person, company, or community?"
- "What does the other side actually gain or lose here, beyond what they say?"
- "If you 'win' this round, what does it cost you in the next ten?"
- "Is this someone you want to be playing with in twenty years?"
- "Can you test this for a month, or at a tenth of the cost, before committing fully?"
- "What single result, decided now, would tell you the experiment worked?"

## Failure mode

Game theory assumes you know the other side's payoffs, and you usually do not. Treating friends, family, or coworkers as calculating players can turn a cooperative relationship competitive just through how you frame it. Use it to understand structure, not to become calculating.
