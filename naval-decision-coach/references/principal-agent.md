# The Principal-Agent Problem

**The question it answers**: Why does this look right on paper but go wrong in practice? Can I trust this advice?

**One-line summary**: People optimize for their own payoff, not yours. Follow the incentive.

## What it really says

- **Principal**: the person who owns the outcome, good or bad. It is their money, their career, their project, their life.
- **Agent**: someone who acts for the principal but does not own the result (an advisor, employee, consultant, broker, recruiter, salesperson, even a well-meaning friend).

The problem is structural, not moral. Two ingredients create it:

1. **Information asymmetry**: the agent knows things the principal cannot easily check (how hard they worked, what the real risk is, what the alternatives are).
2. **Misaligned payoff**: the agent is rewarded for something narrower than the outcome (this quarter's number, a commission, a fee, avoiding blame).

Remove either ingredient and the problem shrinks. Remove neither and no amount of goodwill fixes it. Even honest people drift toward their own metric.

**Naval's version**: when you are the principal, you own the outcome; when you are an agent, you are paid for your time. This is why he pushes toward equity and ownership: it is the most reliable way to make your payoff the same shape as the result.

**Taleb's version**: do not take advice from someone who does not have skin in the game.

**Munger's version**: "Show me the incentive and I will show you the outcome."

**The key question for every piece of advice: "What happens to this person if they are wrong?"**

## Where the gap opens

The principal wants long-term value. The agent often wants this quarter's metric, the bonus, the commission, or career safety. The principal pays for the gap.

**Four ways to close it**:
1. **Skin in the game**: equity, deferred pay, shared downside.
2. **Measure outcomes**: not activity, and not only the short term.
3. **Shorten the chain**: fewer agents between you and the result.
4. **Do it yourself**: when the stakes are too high to delegate.

## Where it shows up

- **The advisor**: a financial advisor paid by commission is rewarded for transactions, not your returns. The advice may be honest and the incentive still points the wrong way.
- **The employee or employer**: paid for time and judged on a narrow metric, so effort flows to what is measured.
- **The vendor or consultant**: paid for the project, not for the result. A longer engagement is worth more to them than a fast fix.
- **The salesperson, recruiter, or real estate agent**: paid when a deal closes, not when it is the right deal for you.
- **Friends and family**: love you, but may also want you nearby, safe, or making choices that reflect well on them. Their downside is not your downside.
- **You, as an agent**: where are you optimizing for your own metric at the expense of the person you serve? Being honest about this is how you become someone others trust.

## Real examples to draw on

- **Wells Fargo (2016)**: aggressive cross-selling targets pushed staff to open millions of accounts customers never asked for. The bank paid huge fines and lost its reputation. Employees were responding rationally to the incentive. The failure was in the design, not the character of thousands of people.
- **The real estate agent study (Steven Levitt)**: agents kept their own homes on the market longer and sold them for about 3 percent more than clients' homes. On your house, extra days of effort earn the agent a tiny share; on theirs, they keep it all.
- **Profit sharing in Taiwan's tech sector**: making engineers shareholders turned agents into partial principals and helped retain the talent that built the industry. Alignment was bought with ownership.

Each outcome was predictable from the payoff structure alone, without knowing anyone involved.

## How to apply it to a decision

1. **Name the principal and the agents.** Who owns this outcome (usually the person asking)? Who is advising, acting for, or influencing them?
2. **Write down each party's actual payoff.** What gets them paid, promoted, praised, or protected? Use real compensation, not stated intentions.
3. **Find the gap.** Look for situations where the agent wins and the person loses. If none appears, check the timing; many gaps only appear over a longer horizon.
4. **Close it with skin in the game first.** Fee-only rather than commission, payment tied to results, shared downside. Alignment beats supervision.
5. **Where alignment is impossible, redesign.** Get a second opinion from someone paid differently, measure outcomes rather than activity, shorten the chain, or keep the decision yourself.
6. **Turn the model on yourself.** Where is the person acting as an agent for someone else, and is that shaping this decision?

## Diagnostic questions to ask the person

- "Who has advised you on this, and what happens to each of them if they are wrong?"
- "Who gets paid, or gets something they want, if you choose option A? Option B?"
- "Have you asked anyone who has nothing to gain either way?"
- "In this option, do you own the result, or are you renting out your time?"
- "Is there a way to get the advisor's payoff tied to your result?"

## Failure mode

Principal-agent thinking breeds cynicism. Most people are not gaming you, and constant suspicion is expensive and damages relationships. **Use it to weigh advice and design arrangements, not to interrogate the motives of people who care about you.** An incentive gap means "discount and verify," not "they are lying."
