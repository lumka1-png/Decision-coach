# Compound Interest

**The question it answers**: Why do small, boring, repeated actions beat large, dramatic ones? Which path is better over the long run?

**One-line summary**: Gains that earn more gains grow exponentially. Time is the exponent, not the rate.

## What it really says

Compounding does not simply mean "things add up." It means each gain becomes part of the base that produces the next gain. The output feeds back into the input. That feedback loop turns a small, ordinary rate into a result that looks impossible from the starting line.

**The counterintuitive part**: time matters more than rate. Doubling your rate of return doubles your result. Doubling your time horizon squares it. That is why the curve looks flat for years and then seems to explode. Nothing changed except that the base got large. **Most people quit during the flat part, which is exactly where the whole return is decided.**

Naval's version: "All the returns in life, whether in wealth, relationships, or knowledge, come from compound interest."

## What actually compounds (and what does not)

Compounds:
- **Capital**, because returns earn returns.
- **Knowledge and skills**, because new ideas connect to old ones.
- **Reputation**, because trust lowers the cost of every future deal.
- **Relationships and audience**, because each good interaction makes the next one more likely.
- Also: a codebase, a body of work, health habits.

Does not compound:
- **A salary**, or any work that resets to zero when you stop. If it stops when you stop, it is a wage, not an asset.

The same curve runs in reverse: credit card debt, technical debt, neglected health, and relationships left cold all compound downward. Ignoring something small for ten years is a decision, not the absence of one.

## Useful numbers

- 1.01 to the power of 365 = 37.8. One percent better every day is about 37 times better in a year.
- 0.99 to the power of 365 = 0.03. One percent worse every day leaves almost nothing.
- **Rule of 72**: divide 72 by the growth rate to get the doubling time. At 10 percent, the base doubles about every 7 years.
- Talent × effort = skill. Skill × effort = achievement. (Angela Duckworth's formulation, used in the course.) Effort counts twice, so it matters more than talent.
- Example: saving 10,000 a month at 7 percent becomes about 1.7 million in 10 years and about 5.2 million in 20. The second decade produces roughly three times what the first did, from identical deposits.

## Real examples to draw on

- **Warren Buffett**: about 20 percent a year, which is excellent but not magical. What nobody matched was doing it for more than 50 years without interruption. Most of his wealth came after age 50. His rule against permanent loss exists to protect the base, because one wipeout multiplies every prior gain by zero.
- **TSMC**: each process node funded research for the next. Competitors could buy the same equipment but not thirty years of accumulated process knowledge.
- **Toyota and kaizen**: thousands of tiny improvements per year, each written down as the new baseline.
- **Naval**: treats knowledge, reputation, and relationships as the accounts that compound fastest. "Play long-term games with long-term people" is compounding applied to who you spend time with.
- **Stephen King**: about 2,000 words a day, for decades. Consistency, not intensity.

## How to apply it to a decision

1. **Identify which option builds a compounding asset.** Capital, a skill, a reputation, an audience, a relationship. Which option is a wage that resets?
2. **Check the rate is sustainable.** A pace you can hold for ten years beats one you give up on in three months. Sustainability is a mathematical requirement here, not a lifestyle preference.
3. **Protect the base above all.** Never risk ruin. Survival ranks above optimization. Does either option put the base at risk (health, savings, reputation)?
4. **Reinvest the output.** Which option lets the person put gains back in (profits into the business, learning into harder problems, goodwill into the relationship)?
5. **Extend the horizon and stop interrupting.** Switching field, strategy, or partners every two years restarts the curve. Does this option restart the curve? Is the restart worth it?
6. **Expect the flat years.** Judge the direction, not the week. Is the person about to quit during the flat part?

## Diagnostic questions to ask the person

- "Does today's work on this make tomorrow's work easier, or does it start again at zero?"
- "If you keep doing this for ten years, what will you have built that you own?"
- "Is the thing you're being patient with actually growing, or just continuing?"
- "Which option has a small chance of wiping out everything you have built so far?"
- "Are you judging this after a few weeks, when the curve is supposed to look flat?"

## Failure mode

Compound interest makes people patient with things that do not compound. A dead-end role, a failing product, and a cold relationship do not improve with time. **Patience only pays when the underlying asset is actually growing. Check that first.**
