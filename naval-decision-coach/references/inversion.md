# Inversion

**The question it answers**: We cannot agree on the best plan, or I'm afraid this could go wrong. What now?

**One-line summary**: Stop asking how to succeed. Ask what would guarantee failure, then remove those causes.

## What it really says

"Man muss immer umkehren" (one must always invert). The mathematician Carl Jacobi solved hard problems by restating them backward. Charlie Munger turned that habit into a decision rule for business and life: do not ask how to win, ask what would guarantee losing, and then do not do those things.

**Why backward is easier**:
- The forward question ("How do we succeed?") has an unlimited number of vague answers and no way to test them.
- The backward question ("What would kill this?") has a short list of specific answers that everyone can already name.
- Avoiding a known failure is a concrete action with an owner and a date. Pursuing abstract success is not.

**The deeper claim**: you cannot reliably cause success, because it depends on timing, competitors, and luck you do not control. You can reliably remove causes of failure, because most of them are internal and boring: running out of cash, losing key people, ignoring the customer, taking on too much debt. **Avoiding stupidity is more repeatable than seeking brilliance.**

Inversion also breaks deadlock. People rarely agree on the best plan. They agree almost instantly on what would be fatal.

| Forward: "How do we succeed?" | Inverted: "What guarantees failure?" |
|---|---|
| Hundreds of possible answers, all plausible | Six to ten specific answers, named in minutes |
| No way to test them, so people argue | Each one is testable and has an owner |
| Vague plan, slow agreement | Concrete rules, fast agreement |

## Where it shows up

- **Strategy**: instead of "How do we grow revenue 30 percent?", ask "What would make us lose our three biggest customers?"
- **Hiring**: instead of "What makes a great hire?", ask "What has every failed hire here had in common?"
- **Projects (the pre-mortem)**: "It is twelve months from now and the project failed completely. Write the story of how."
- **Personal life**: instead of "How do I become happy?", ask "What reliably makes me miserable?" Then remove those things.
- **Relationships**: instead of "How do I make this work?", ask "What would definitely destroy this?"
- **Money**: instead of "How do I get rich?", ask "What would make me broke?" (debt, no emergency fund, one concentrated bet, lifestyle inflation).

## Real examples to draw on

- **Munger's 1986 "how to be miserable" speech**: instead of describing a good life, it gave precise instructions for a miserable one: be unreliable, be envious, be resentful, and learn only from your own experience. It is widely quoted because avoidance instructions are actionable.
- **Berkshire's avoidance rules**: no heavy leverage, nothing outside their circle of competence, no bet that risks the company. That is why they were still solvent and buying in 2000 and 2008.
- **Aviation safety**: did not become safe by studying successful flights. It became safe by investigating every crash and turning each cause into a permanent checklist item.
- **Gary Klein's pre-mortem**: assuming the project has already failed produces much more honest and specific risk discussion than asking "what might go wrong?", because it removes the social cost of predicting failure.
- **Munger's second rule**: you are not entitled to an opinion until you can argue the other side better than its supporters can.

## How to apply it to a decision (the 30-minute procedure)

1. **State the goal in one plain sentence.** No jargon. If the goal is fuzzy, fix that first.
2. **Ask the inverted question without filtering.** "It is one year from now and this went badly. What happened?" Collect at least ten causes before judging any. Do this for each option, including the option of doing nothing.
3. **Sort by likelihood and damage.** A likely, survivable problem is different from an unlikely, fatal one. **Deal with the fatal ones first, whatever their probability.**
4. **Turn the top three into avoidance rules.** Each becomes a concrete rule, ideally with a date or a number. "I will keep six months of expenses in savings before I quit" is a rule. "Be careful with money" is not.
5. **Return to the forward question.** Inversion protects a plan; it does not create one. Once the fatal causes are handled, build the plan.
6. **Re-run the list regularly.** New failure modes appear as things change. An old list gives false comfort.

## Steelman check

Before finalizing, argue the opposite option as strongly as its best supporter would. If you cannot, you do not yet understand the decision well enough to recommend against it.

## Diagnostic questions to ask the person

- "Imagine it's a year from now and you deeply regret this choice. What went wrong?"
- "What is the one thing that, if it happened, you could not recover from?"
- "What reliably makes you miserable? Does either option bring more of it?"
- "What has gone wrong the last few times you made a similar decision?"
- "What would someone who disagrees with you say, at their most convincing?"

## Failure mode

Used alone, inversion produces caution and nothing else. A list of things to avoid is not a strategy, and it can talk people out of every worthwhile risk. **Invert to protect the plan, then return to the forward question to build it.**
